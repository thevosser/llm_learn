## The concept

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus ran a simple, brutal experiment on
himself: memorize something, then test his own recall at increasing time
intervals afterward. The result — replicated many times since — is the
"forgetting curve": retention drops fast at first, then more slowly, but
it drops a lot. A large chunk of what you "learned" today is typically
gone within a day or two without any further contact with it, and most
of the rest follows within a couple of weeks.

That would be discouraging except for the second half of the finding: a
single well-timed review resets the curve — and each subsequent review
resets it more, and the curve gets flatter each time. This is spaced
repetition. It's not about reviewing more often overall; it's about
reviewing right around the point you're about to forget, rather than
either too soon (when it's still fresh, so the review teaches you
nothing new) or too late (after it's already gone, so you're relearning
it from scratch instead of reinforcing it).

The mechanism underneath the review itself matters too: actively trying
to recall something from memory (retrieval practice) strengthens it far
more than passively re-reading it, even though re-reading feels more
comfortable and "review-y" in the moment. Struggling to remember and
succeeding is doing real work; smoothly re-reading familiar material
mostly isn't.

## Why it matters

This is the most directly actionable idea in this whole set for
improving the project itself: right now, every lesson is "covered" once
and never revisited — there's no mechanism that brings an older topic
back around for a quick retrieval check before it's actually forgotten.
A genuinely valuable next feature would be exactly that: periodically
resurfacing a short recall check on something marked "covered" a week or
two ago, timed to land right where the forgetting curve says it's needed
most.
