## The concept

The idea that people are "visual learners" or "auditory learners" or
"kinesthetic learners" — and learn best when material is matched to that
style — is one of the most widely believed ideas in education. It's also
one of the most thoroughly tested and failed. Decades of controlled
studies have looked for the specific claim the theory makes: that matching
teaching format to someone's preferred style improves *outcomes*, not just
comfort. That effect essentially doesn't show up. People do have format
preferences, and those preferences feel real and consistent — but
preferring to watch something doesn't mean you actually retain more of it
than you would from doing it hands-on.

What the research actually does support is much less flattering to any
one preferred style and much more useful: certain *methods* of engaging
with material reliably improve retention for basically everyone,
regardless of which format they say they prefer. Actively retrieving
information from memory beats re-reading it. Doing something yourself
beats watching someone else do it. Spacing practice out over time beats
cramming it into one sitting. None of that is about matching a style — 
it's about what the brain does with information regardless of how it
first arrived.

So the useful question was never "what's my learning style" — it was
always "which of these known-effective methods am I actually using."

## Why it matters

This directly shapes what this project should optimize for. Offering the
same lesson as a video *and* a podcast *and* a diagram to match different
"styles" wouldn't move the needle — the research says so. What would move
it: making sure every lesson uses the methods that actually work
(retrieval, doing, spacing), which is exactly the territory the next few
lessons dig into, and exactly why this project was built around editable,
interactive exercises instead of passive content in the first place.
