## The concept

Working memory — the mental scratch space you use to actively think about
something — can hold roughly three to five distinct chunks of information
at once before it starts dropping things. Cognitive load theory is built
on that limit: the moment new material demands more active juggling than
working memory can hold, comprehension doesn't degrade gracefully, it
falls off a cliff. You're not "a little confused," you're not
tracking the material at all anymore, and everything after that point
mostly bounces off.

A long, dense session runs into this constantly. Even if each individual
idea is simple, stacking several new ideas in a row without a break to
consolidate any of them means working memory is perpetually overloaded by
the second or third concept, regardless of how clearly the first one was
explained. Length doesn't just fail to add extra learning past a certain
point — it actively hurts what came before it, because overloaded working
memory doesn't get a chance to hand anything off to longer-term memory.

Short, focused sessions work with this limit instead of against it: one
idea (or a genuinely small cluster of related ones), enough time to
actually engage with it, then stop before load builds up. This is also
why cramming a subject the night before a test produces such fragile
recall — it's the same overload problem, just compressed into a few
hours instead of spread across a semester's worth of dense lectures.

## Why it matters

This is a direct validation of a decision already baked into this
project's design: HANDOFF specifies lessons should be "bite-sized (5-15
min)" on purpose, not as a compromise for a busy schedule. Cognitive load
theory is the actual mechanism behind why that constraint helps rather
than just accommodates — and it's a strong argument against ever letting a
single lesson's scope creep past what fits comfortably in one sitting.
