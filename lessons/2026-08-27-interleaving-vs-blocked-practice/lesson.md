## The concept

"Blocked" practice means working through many examples of the same type
in a row before moving to the next type — AAAA, then BBBB, then CCCC.
"Interleaved" practice means mixing types together instead — ABC, BCA,
CAB. Blocked practice feels smoother, and performance *during* the
practice session is genuinely better with it — but that's misleading,
because most of what's driving that smooth performance is momentum from
the nearly-identical previous problem, not real discrimination between
types.

Interleaved practice feels worse while it's happening — more effortful,
more mistakes, less of that satisfying streak of getting things right in
a row. But on a delayed test, especially one that requires figuring out
*which* approach applies before you can even use it (not just executing a
method you were already told to use), interleaved practice reliably wins,
often by a large margin. The extra effort of constantly having to
re-identify "which type of problem is this" turns out to be exactly the
skill blocked practice never trains at all.

This connects directly to the fluency-illusion idea from earlier lessons:
blocked practice produces a *feeling* of mastery during practice that
doesn't transfer, while interleaved practice produces a worse feeling in
the moment and a better result later — a mismatch between how something
feels while you're doing it and how well it actually worked.

## Why it matters

This is a second, independent argument (alongside the forgetting-curve
lesson's case for spaced review) for a feature this project doesn't have
yet: right now, every lesson covers exactly one isolated topic, in
isolation, never mixed back in with earlier ones. A future review
mechanism built around interleaving several older topics together —
rather than one clean topic at a time — would be doing something blocked,
single-topic lessons structurally can't.
