## The concept

Watching someone else explain or demonstrate something produces a
specific and misleading feeling: fluency. The explanation makes sense as
you follow along, the steps seem clear, and that smoothness gets
misread as "I understand this now." The problem is that following along
with something and being able to reproduce or apply it yourself are
different skills, and passive watching only trains the first one.

This is sometimes called the "fluency illusion" — and it's strongest
exactly when the material is well-explained and easy to follow, which is
the cruel part. A confusing lecture at least signals "I don't get this
yet." A crisp, well-produced tutorial video signals the same false
confidence whether or not anything transferred into your own ability to
do the thing without the video playing.

Active learning breaks that illusion by forcing the gap to show up early:
edit the code yourself and it either runs or it doesn't: no ambiguity
about whether you actually understood the syntax. Answer a question from
memory instead of recognizing the answer in a multiple-choice list, and
you find out immediately whether you can actually produce the idea, not
just recognize it when it's handed to you. It feels harder and slower in
the moment — which is also exactly why it works better; the effort is the
mechanism, not a side effect to minimize.

## Why it matters

This isn't abstract for this project — it's the literal design principle
already written into HANDOFF: "lessons must always be genuinely
interactive — never 'here's a link, go read/watch it.'" That rule wasn't
a style preference, it's this exact research. Every lesson requiring you
to click a toggle, drag a slider, or edit and run real code instead of
just reading prose is the active-learning bet made concrete.
