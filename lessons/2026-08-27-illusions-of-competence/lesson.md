## The concept

Rereading notes, highlighting a passage, watching a clear explanation —
all of these produce a strong, immediate feeling: recognition, "yes, this
makes sense, I follow this." That feeling is fluency, and it gets
misread constantly as "I know this now." Fluency and actually-retrievable
knowledge are measurably different things, and the gap between them has
a name: an illusion of competence.

The uncomfortable part of the research here is which methods produce the
strongest illusions: precisely the ones that feel the most productive.
Rereading, highlighting, and watching all generate high fluency with
relatively little real retrieval effort, so they feel efficient and
confidence-inspiring — which is exactly why people keep choosing them
even after being told they're weak, and keep avoiding methods (closed-book
retrieval practice, explaining something from scratch) that feel far less
confidence-inspiring but produce dramatically better actual results.

The practical fix isn't complicated, even if it's uncomfortable: stop
trusting the "does this feel familiar" signal as evidence of learning,
and replace it with an actual test — closed-book, no peeking, right now —
before deciding something is understood.
